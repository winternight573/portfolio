The Glad Game

Designing a Transformational Game for Metacognitive Reflection.

Role Game Designer
Team Solo Project · Educational Game Design
Tools Unity, Ink
Links Project Doc

01 Overview

Starting with a Transformation

Unlike entertainment games that begin with mechanics or fantasy, this project began with a desired change in the player.

I wanted to design a game that helps people become more aware of their own thinking. Rather than simply teaching cognitive biases as facts, the goal was to encourage players to pause, examine their thoughts, and recognize when they might be influenced by distorted thinking patterns.

Throughout the semester, I explored how game mechanics could support this transformation. The project evolved through domain research, interviews with subject matter experts, paper prototyping, playtesting, and multiple design pivots before arriving at the final prototype.

03 Defining the Design Problem

The project began by identifying a specific transformational goal rather than a game mechanic.

Many people are generally aware that cognitive biases exist, yet rarely recognize them while they are actively thinking. Existing resources such as books and self-help materials explain these concepts well, but often rely on passive reading rather than repeated practice.

The design question therefore became:

How might a game help players actively recognize distorted thinking patterns instead of simply learning their names?

To better understand this problem space, I explored literature on metacognition, cognitive psychology, reflective practice, Positive Intelligence, CBT, and educational game design. I also interviewed a mental coach, whose description of awareness, naming, choosing, and shifting became an important framework for the project.

04 Narrowing the Design Space

One of the biggest challenges was determining what transformation was realistic within the scope of a prototype.

Early concepts explored broad themes including lifelong learning, assertiveness, systems thinking, and metacognition. While interesting individually, these ideas proved difficult to measure or translate into concrete gameplay.

Through surveys, conversations, and early concept testing, I gradually narrowed the focus to a much smaller behavioral objective:

Help players practice recognizing distorted thinking.

This shift dramatically simplified both assessment and gameplay design. Rather than attempting to change beliefs or habits directly, the game focused on a single observable skill that could be repeatedly practiced.

05 From Knowledge to Gameplay

The next challenge was translating psychology into mechanics.

Several prototypes emphasized reading, reflection, or dialogue analysis. Although these communicated information effectively, they often felt more like quizzes than games.

Through successive iterations, I explored mechanics that required players to actively inspect language instead of selecting predefined answers. This eventually led to the sentence dissection mechanic, where players identify the specific words contributing to a distorted thought before receiving immediate feedback and explanation.

The educational content became something players performed rather than consumed.

06 Iterating the Experience

The project changed substantially over the semester.

One early prototype cast the player as a life coach helping clients identify biases through dialogue. While playtests showed that players engaged with the psychological analysis, they struggled to emotionally connect with the characters, and the amount of narrative content required made the design difficult to sustain.

Searching for a stronger emotional foundation, I shifted to adapting Pollyanna. The novel's central idea of intentionally reframing negative situations aligned naturally with the transformational goal while providing an existing cast of emotionally grounded characters. This allowed development effort to focus on gameplay rather than inventing an entirely new narrative world.

At the same time, I redesigned the interface around swipe-based dialogue inspired by Reigns and Lapse, making long-form reading feel more interactive while maintaining a lightweight implementation.

07 Playtesting and Design Decisions

Each prototype investigated a different design question rather than simply measuring whether players liked the game.

Design Question Observation Design Decision
Can players recognize distorted thinking? Players often understood the concept but struggled to identify specific wording. Highlight individual words rather than entire sentences.
Does dialogue alone maintain engagement? Players analyzed carefully but found conversations static. Introduced swipe-based dialogue.
Do players remember bias terminology? Players recognized examples more easily than names. Delay explicit terminology until after practice.
What makes players care about learning? Stronger emotional investment increased engagement. Adapted the Pollyanna narrative.

08 Final Prototype

The final prototype combines narrative dialogue with repeated opportunities to identify distorted thinking.

Players experience the story of Pollyanna through swipe-based conversations. During key moments, dialogue transitions into a sentence analysis minigame where players identify the words contributing to biased thinking. Successfully recognizing those patterns reduces a shared negativity meter while introducing cognitive bias terminology through a recurring mentor character.

Rather than presenting psychology as a lesson, the prototype embeds repeated recognition practice directly into the game's progression.

Reflection

This project fundamentally changed how I think about educational games.

Initially, I approached transformational design as a problem of delivering knowledge. Through research and repeated iteration, I came to see transformation as something players must actively practice rather than passively receive. That realization shifted my focus from explaining concepts toward designing interactions that repeatedly exercise the desired skill.

More broadly, the project taught me how to move from an ambitious research question to a focused design problem. By narrowing the intended transformation, validating assumptions through playtesting, and treating prototypes as experiments, I developed a design process that begins with the player change I hope to create and works backward toward mechanics that make that change possible.
