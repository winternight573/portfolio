Big Picture Studio

Designing a Cooperative Puzzle-Narrative Across Four Mobile Screens.

Role Game Designer · Technical Producer

Links Site · Walkthrough

01 Overview

Exploring a New Interaction Medium

Big Picture Studio began with a simple question: if four phones could be physically connected and rearranged, what kinds of games become possible?

Rather than adapting an existing game to multiple screens, our team set out to discover the interaction language of this new medium. Throughout the semester, we investigated how puzzle mechanics, comic-inspired storytelling, and physical phone movement could work together to create an experience that felt both intuitive and unlike any existing mobile game.

The final experience follows Naiad, an explorer investigating the ruins of a forgotten civilization. By physically rearranging four phones, players uncover hidden spaces, solve environmental puzzles, and reconstruct fragments of Earth's forgotten history.

03 Determining the Interaction Language

Single Phone

Personal · Touchscreen · Contained

↓

Four Phones

Shared · Physical · Reconfigurable

Our first design goal was to determine what interactions should define this new medium.

Phones already come with strong player expectations. People know how to tap, swipe, rotate, and hold them. Our challenge was deciding which of those conventions should remain, and which should be replaced with entirely new interactions built around physically connecting multiple devices.

Throughout development, I focused on understanding how players interpreted each interaction through structured playtesting. Rather than asking whether an interaction "worked," we observed why players succeeded or struggled, and whether they naturally transferred existing phone knowledge into this new space.

One important design decision emerged from this process. Early prototypes intentionally minimized familiar touchscreen interactions so that physical phone movement would become the primary mechanic. However, repeated playtests showed that completely abandoning existing conventions made the experience harder to learn. We ultimately reintroduced tapping alongside connecting, rotating, and rearranging phones, creating a balance between familiarity and novelty.

Because the interaction space itself was unfamiliar, our next design goal was to determine how players gradually construct a mental model of the system.

I planned playtests around progressively larger questions. We first examined whether players could perform individual interactions such as connecting and rotating phones. We then explored whether they could transfer that understanding across puzzles, recognize recurring mechanics, and eventually collaborate naturally without prompting.

These observations informed the sequencing of mechanics throughout the experience. Rather than introducing unrelated puzzles, each interaction was designed to reinforce previously learned concepts before introducing a new one.

05 Determining the Relationship Between Gameplay and Narrative

From the beginning, we wanted gameplay and narrative to reinforce one another rather than compete for the player's attention.

Our playtests therefore asked a second question: What story do players understand while solving puzzles?

Players generally understood that they were exploring the ruins of a forgotten civilization, but they sometimes interpreted puzzle interactions literally rather than connecting them to the story. One memorable player summarized the experience as:

"Astronaut talking via erratic smartphones."

This observation suggested that solving an unfamiliar puzzle already occupied most of the player's attention. Working closely with our narrative designer, we restructured the experience so that major story beats occurred immediately after puzzle completion through comic-inspired action sequences. Separating puzzle solving from story delivery reduced cognitive load while making each puzzle feel like it had a meaningful narrative payoff.

As the interaction language became more established, our focus shifted toward determining how mechanics should build upon one another across the entire experience.

I explored progression structures that connected mechanics, inventory, and narrative. Rather than treating puzzles as isolated challenges, we investigated how previously learned interactions could reappear in new contexts, gradually increasing complexity while reinforcing player understanding.

This exploration informed our inventory and key-lock progression, where collected objects such as the watch, camera, and compass became both narrative artifacts and gameplay mechanics. Instead of simply unlocking the next puzzle, each item represented another piece of Naiad's understanding of the world and prepared players for increasingly interconnected interactions later in the game.

07 Learning Through Playtesting

Because there were few existing references for designing across four connected phones, many of our most important decisions came directly from observing players.

Throughout the semester I planned playtests that investigated questions ranging from interaction thresholds and multiplayer collaboration to onboarding clarity and narrative comprehension. Each playtest focused on a specific design question, allowing the team to iterate intentionally rather than relying on intuition alone.

Some examples include:

Design Question What We Learned Design Decision
Is pinch intuitive? Players quickly understood physical connection after one demonstration. Reduced tutorial complexity.
Do players naturally rotate phones? Rotation felt intuitive when visual expectations matched the interaction. Expanded rotation-based mechanics.
Should we remove familiar phone interactions? Existing phone conventions actually supported learning. Reintroduced tapping.
Do players understand the story while solving puzzles? Puzzle solving competed with narrative comprehension. Moved story beats after puzzle completion.

08 Communicating the Project

Because our interaction space could only truly be understood by playing it, communicating the project became an important design challenge in itself.

I designed and maintained the team's public website, documenting our exploration process through weekly development logs. Rather than simply recording progress, these posts explained the questions we were trying to answer, the prototypes we built, and the design decisions that followed each playtest.

The website also became a valuable communication tool during faculty reviews, GDC, and Festival of Gaming, helping audiences understand both the experience and the thinking behind it before they ever picked up the phones.

09 Final Experience

After months of exploration, iteration, and playtesting, Big Picture Studio culminated in a complete cooperative puzzle-narrative where physical phone movement, puzzle solving, comic storytelling, and visual design came together as a single cohesive experience.

What began as an exploration of a novel interaction space ultimately became an experience about discovering how familiar technology can support entirely new forms of shared play.

Reflection

Big Picture Studio changed how I think about game design.

Rather than asking what mechanics would be interesting, I learned to ask how players build understanding. Every interaction, puzzle, and narrative beat became an opportunity to teach, reinforce, or expand a player's mental model of the game. Designing for a new medium required balancing novelty with familiarity, continually validating assumptions through playtesting, and refining the experience until the interaction itself became invisible and the story could take center stage.
